### Reading: The Rain Has a Song (192 words)

Sione watched rain fall outside his classroom. Water ran down the window in crooked lines. He wrote, “It is raining outside.” His teacher said that sentence was prose: ordinary writing that tells the reader something. Then she asked him to notice the sounds. Sione listened. Rain tapped on the roof. Shoes splashed across the path. A bus hissed as it stopped by the gate. He wrote three new lines: “Rain taps a song on the roof. Puddles clap beneath our shoes. Grey clouds cover the playground.” His friend Aroha read the lines and smiled. “I can hear the rain,” she said. Sione noticed that the new writing was shorter but helped her imagine more. He had chosen particular words and placed each idea on its own line. The poem did not need to rhyme. It needed to help the reader see or hear what mattered. At the bottom he wrote a title: “Rain at School.”  Sione drew a small picture beside his lines. He noticed that the drawing showed the puddles, while the words helped him hear them. He told Aroha that pictures and poems could both help people imagine a moment.

### Reading: More Than Just Words (337 words)

Mele found a page of song lyrics in her cousin's notebook. She recognised the words but noticed that they sounded different when her cousin performed them. Pauses, repeated sounds and emphasis gave the lines a shape that ordinary prose did not have. Later, her grandfather invited her into the garden. He showed her a young plant growing beside an older tree. “A small beginning can become something strong,” he said. Mele wrote down the sentence. At first she thought it was only advice about gardening. Then she realised it might describe a person learning something difficult. She drafted a poem in which a young plant stood for courage. Her first version explained every connection: “The plant is like me because I am learning to be brave.” Her cousin suggested leaving room for the reader to think. Mele revised it: “Beside the old tree, one green leaf lifts towards the light.” The line showed an action without explaining every possible meaning. At school, classmates interpreted the leaf in different ways. One saw confidence. Another saw hope after a hard week. Mele was surprised that both readings could be supported by the words. She concluded that poetry used careful language, rhythm and images to invite thought, not merely to decorate ordinary sentences.  Mele tested another opening that described the garden as a classroom. It explained the lesson too directly, so she removed it. She wanted the plant to remain a real plant as well as a possible symbol. Her grandfather liked that the poem still mentioned the damp soil and the crooked stem. Without those details, he said, the leaf might have felt like an idea rather than something they had seen together.  She also noticed that her cousin's spoken performance used a pause before the final image. The pause gave listeners time to picture the leaf. On the page, she placed that image on its own line. She was beginning to understand that a poet could shape not only the words but also the way readers met them.

### Reading: The Space Between the Lines (433 words)

Jayden's older brother was leaving Auckland to study at university. The family was proud, but the house felt strange while everyone packed. Jayden tried to write about the departure. His first paragraph listed facts: the suitcase was black, the bus left on Sunday and his brother had promised to call. The paragraph was accurate, yet it did not communicate the mixture of excitement and loss. Jayden began again with an image: “An empty chair waits at the kitchen table.” He did not mean the chair could think. He meant that its emptiness would remind the family of someone they loved. He placed a short line after it: “Tomorrow.” The pause seemed important. It made the coming change feel close. His teacher asked why he had not written a long explanation of his feelings. Jayden said the chair allowed readers to recognise absence through an ordinary object. They discussed how line breaks, images and silence could guide a reader's attention. He removed a line about the bus timetable because it interrupted the mood. He kept the detail of his brother's old hoodie hanging behind the door; it suggested presence and memory at the same time. When his brother read the finished poem, he did not say much. He folded the page and put it inside his suitcase. Jayden realised that a poem could communicate an experience without stating every emotion directly.  Jayden considered calling the empty chair “lonely”, but the adjective seemed to tell readers what to feel. He preferred the quieter image of a chair waiting. His mother read the poem and noticed the line about the hoodie. She said the small object captured the way someone could seem present even when they were away. Jayden moved that image closer to the ending. He wanted the poem to end with connection, not only with loss. The arrangement of lines became as important as the words themselves.  In class, Jayden compared his poem with the original factual paragraph. The paragraph answered what happened and when; the poem explored how the change felt. Neither form was automatically better. Each served a different purpose. He noticed that the short line “Tomorrow” interrupted the rhythm, just as the departure interrupted family routines. When he tried placing the word at the end of a long sentence, the effect weakened. He restored the line break. Jayden also asked whether the empty chair image was too familiar. His teacher said originality mattered, but precision mattered more. The chair belonged to his family's actual kitchen, so it carried a specific memory rather than an invented decoration. He chose to keep it.

### Reading: The Weight of a Single Word (593 words)

Mele discovered her grandfather's old radio in a cupboard after he died. The dial was scratched and the handle had been repaired with tape. She remembered the sound of news broadcasts drifting through the kitchen while he made tea. When she tried to write about him, she produced a paragraph of factual memories. It was accurate, but she felt that the sentences placed too much distance between the reader and the experience. She began a poem with the line, “The radio keeps a chair for his voice.” The image was impossible literally, yet it gave shape to her feeling that familiar sounds could remain connected to someone who was gone. Her teacher asked why she chose “keeps” rather than “holds”. Mele said “keeps” suggested care and a decision not to let something disappear. She tested “guards” as an alternative, but that made the radio seem defensive. She returned to “keeps”. In the next stanza she described a small click as the dial turned. That detail mattered more to her than the radio's colour, because the click brought back the beginning of every evening broadcast. She placed a line break before the word “silence”, allowing the reader to experience a pause. A classmate interpreted the radio as a symbol of family continuity. Another saw it as a reminder that technology can preserve traces of a voice but not restore the person. Mele recognised that both interpretations fitted the poem. She had not used rhyme, and she had not explained the meaning of every image. Instead, she had selected details, tested the connotations of words and shaped the space around them. The poem was not a complete account of her grandfather's life. It was an invitation to understand one part of her memory. She titled it “Between Stations”, because the phrase referred both to the radio and to the uncertain space between loss and remembering.  During revision, Mele also questioned whether the radio could stand for every memory of her grandfather. She decided it could not, and that limitation was useful. The poem would be strongest if it focused on one relationship between an object and a remembered voice. She compared two endings. One explained that she missed him; the other ended with the dial settling between stations. The second gave readers more room to consider what silence might mean. She retained the image but kept the preceding details concrete so the ending did not become obscure. The next morning she read the poem aloud. She heard a repeated consonant sound in “click” and “cupboard” that made the opening sharper. She left it in, but removed a forced rhyme with “night” because it sounded sentimental. The final version was less decorative and more faithful to her memory.  Mele considered how readers unfamiliar with her grandfather might understand the poem. They would not know his favourite programmes or the way he adjusted the volume, so she included one precise gesture: he turned the dial slowly with two fingers until the static cleared. That action made the scene visible without requiring background knowledge. She also revised the order of the stanzas. The first now presented the object, the second introduced remembered sound, and the third allowed silence to enter. The progression mirrored the experience of finding the radio and realising what it recalled. A classmate suggested adding an explanation of the family's history, but Mele decided it belonged in a different piece. This poem was about one object and the emotional associations it carried. By narrowing its focus, she made the memory more accessible rather than less.
