### Reading: The Beat at Break Time (194 words)

At morning break, Sione bounced a basketball beside the court. Boom, boom, bounce. Levi tapped his shoes to the beat. Aroha called, “Ball, wall, tall!” The words sounded alike at the end. “That's rhyme,” she said. Sione tried a poem: “The ball goes high. When he read it aloud, the new line felt too long. He cut out three words. Now the beat was easier to follow. His teacher explained that rhythm is the pattern of sounds and stresses in words. He wanted the words to sound good and make sense. They clapped the ending together before running back to play.

### Reading: The Rhythm of Our Street (254 words)

Mele heard a familiar rhythm each afternoon as she walked home. A gate clicked. A dog barked twice. A bus stopped with a long hiss. Children called across the road. She decided to turn the sounds into a poem. Her first version rhymed every pair of lines, but she had to use awkward words to make the endings match. Her cousin read it and asked why a dog was described as a “royal frog”. Mele laughed. She had chosen “frog” only because it rhymed with “dog”. She rewrote the verse, keeping the sound pattern but using words that made sense. “The gate clicks shut. Two paws race by. A bus sighs under the evening sky.” Some lines rhymed; others did not. Mele clapped while reading and noticed that the short line about the gate sounded sharp, while the longer bus line slowed the pace. She understood that rhythm could suggest movement and mood. She chose not to force another rhyme into the ending. Instead, she repeated “home” twice to show where the sounds were leading her. Her final poem felt like her own street, not a collection of words chosen only for their endings.  Her teacher asked the class to tap the beat of two versions. Students could keep time with the shorter opening, but they slowed down during the bus line. Mele said that was exactly what she wanted: the walk home was busy at first, then calmer near her gate. The repeated word “home” made the ending feel settled rather than rushed.

### Reading: When Rhythm Becomes Meaning (618 words)

Mele listened to an old radio recording of a spoken-word performance. The speaker began with a regular beat, then stopped unexpectedly before one important word. The silence drew more attention than a louder voice might have done. Mele wanted to understand why. She marked the stressed syllables in the opening lines and noticed that the pattern made the audience expect a continuation. Breaking the pattern created tension. She experimented with a poem about receiving difficult news. Her first draft used end rhyme in every line. Although the sounds were neat, the predictable pattern made the subject feel strangely light. She removed several rhymes and kept a repeated phrase: “I waited.” Each repetition had a different number of words after it. The phrase became a measure of time rather than a decorative refrain. She then placed a single-word line after a long sentence. Reading it aloud, she noticed how the sudden stop changed her breathing. Her teacher asked whether that effect would remain for a reader who never heard the poem performed. Mele examined the page. Line breaks and punctuation provided visual clues, but they could not control every reader's pace. She added a dash where the pause mattered most and removed one unnecessary comma. A classmate read the poem with a different emphasis and created a slightly different meaning. Mele accepted that rhythm was partly shaped by the reader. They discussed how musicality, repetition, stress and silence could influence interpretation without determining it completely. Mele also compared the sound of her poem with a prose account of the same event. The prose conveyed the sequence efficiently; the poem made the waiting feel prolonged. By the final draft, she understood that rhythm was not simply an ornament or a requirement to rhyme. It was a method of organising emotional experience in time.  For the workshop presentation, Mele asked two classmates to perform the same stanza. One paused after “waited”; the other stressed the final word. Their readings suggested different degrees of resignation. Mele revised the punctuation to make her preferred emphasis more likely, while recognising she could not dictate every performance. She also tested the poem with a quiet spoken delivery rather than an exaggerated dramatic voice. The quieter version made the repeated phrase sound more personal. She concluded that performance choices were part of the poem's interpretation, not an optional layer added after writing.  Mele questioned whether repeated words always strengthened a poem. She found that using “I waited” too often made the refrain lose force. She removed one repetition and left a space where listeners expected it. The absence created its own emphasis. She also tried ending the poem with a neat couplet, but the perfect rhyme suggested an emotional resolution that the experience did not offer. She kept an unrhymed ending. Her teacher observed that deliberate irregularity could be as controlled as a regular beat, provided the writer understood its effect. Mele annotated the final draft with performance choices: slow at the opening, restrained through the middle, and a longer silence after the last line. Those decisions connected the poem's form to its meaning.  At the end, Mele compared the recording with her written poem once more. The recording allowed the performer to control the silence directly, while the page relied on lineation and punctuation to invite a pause. She did not rank one medium above the other. Instead, she revised each for its audience. She also noticed that a listener could hear hesitation in the voice, while a reader might infer it from a broken line. Both experiences depended on rhythm, but they offered different kinds of evidence. That distinction helped her prepare a performance that respected the written poem without simply repeating it mechanically.
