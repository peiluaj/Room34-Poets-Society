# ROOM 34 POETS SOCIETY — WEEK 1 LESSON 5

**Friday Period 3 | 60 minutes | My First Complete Poem**

**Learning intention:** Combine poetry techniques and improve writing through purposeful revision.

**Success criteria:** Identify techniques, write a poem with a message, revise a line and explain a choice.

**Lesson sequence:** Discover 5 min · Spelling 10 min · Reading 25 min · Writing 15 min · Reflection 5 min.

## Discover

Compare the first draft, “The rain was loud. I went home. I felt happy.”, with the revised version: “Rain drums on the classroom roof. My shoes splash through silver puddles. Home is a warm light waiting for me.” Discuss how language choices change the reader’s experience.

## Pioneers

### Reading — Lunchtime Basketball (207 words)

Sione wanted to write a poem about lunchtime basketball. He loved playing with his friends on the school court.
His first draft said:
I play basketball.
My friends are there.
We have fun.
Sione read it twice. The words were true, but they did not help him remember the game. He closed his eyes and listened in his imagination. The ball bounced. His shoes squeaked. His friends shouted his name.
He picked up his pencil and tried again:
The basketball bounces like a drum.
My shoes sing on the court.
Our team is a busy hive.
We race beneath the bright sun.
Now Sione could almost hear the game. He liked the first line because the bouncing ball sounded like a drum. He liked the second because shoes cannot really sing. Giving shoes a human action was personification.
His teacher smiled at the new poem. “You have helped your reader hear and see the game,” she said.
Sione added the title Lunchtime Basketball. He checked his spelling and read the lines aloud one last time. Then he changed “race” to “dash” because it sounded quicker.
His poem was not longer than the first one. It was better because every word helped show what lunchtime basketball felt like.

## Creators

### Reading — Where Summer Lives (262 words)

Aroha opened her notebook to write about the beach where her family spent summer afternoons. Her first draft was short:
The beach is nice.
The water is blue.
My family has fun.
She sighed. Those sentences were true, but they could describe almost any beach. She wanted her cousin Mika to recognise their favourite place.
Aroha closed her eyes. She remembered warm sand beneath her feet, salty air on her lips and her uncle calling everyone back for lunch. Her cousins ran towards the waves, laughing as the water rushed around their ankles.
She wrote a new version:
The ocean is a blue blanket.
Its waves clap against the shore.
Warm sand hugs my tired feet.
Our laughter floats across the sea.
Her teacher read the poem and asked, “What feeling do you want readers to carry away?”
“Belonging,” Aroha replied. “The beach matters because my family is there.”
She added another line: This beach is our summer home.
The beach was not a real house, but the metaphor suggested comfort and shared memories. Aroha then replaced “floats” with “dances”. The new verb made the laughter sound lively and playful.
She read the poem aloud and noticed that the final two lines carried its message. She placed them together at the end. Then she checked her spelling and gave the poem a title: Where Summer Lives.
When Mika heard it, he smiled. “That sounds like our beach,” he said.
Aroha realised that a first draft was only a beginning. Remembering, choosing and changing words had helped her write a poem worth keeping.

## Visionaries

### Reading — Between Home and Tomorrow (340 words)

Mele had written descriptions before, but finishing a complete poem felt different. Her teacher said a strong poem needed more than attractive language. “Find the idea at its heart,” she explained.
Mele chose her morning walk to school. Every day she passed the same dairy, crossed a busy road and walked beneath a row of trees. Her first lines were plain:
I walk to school each day.
Cars drive past.
The trees are green.
She looked at the draft and wondered why it felt empty. Then she remembered that the same journey could feel different each morning. Sometimes she was excited to see her friends. Other mornings she worried about a test or an argument from the day before.
Mele wrote a new opening:
The morning road is a ribbon of questions.
Cars grumble beneath the waking sky.
The trees raise their arms above my head.
The images captured uncertainty, but she wanted the poem to move somewhere. She planned three stages: leaving home, walking through the neighbourhood and reaching the school gate.
At the beginning, the road stretched ahead like a question she could not answer. In the middle, traffic and footsteps crowded her thoughts. Near the end, familiar voices cut through the noise. She added:
My friends call out across the morning.
The school gate becomes an open door.
Her teacher asked what had changed. “At first I am worried,” Mele explained. “By the end I feel ready.”
The two metaphors supported that change. A ribbon of questions suggested uncertainty continuing along the road. An open door suggested welcome and possibility.
Mele read her draft aloud. One sentence was too long and made her stumble, so she shortened it. She also removed a line about a barking dog. Although it was a good detail, it did not help explain her emotional journey.
She chose the title Between Home and Tomorrow. The poem was about an ordinary walk, but it now carried a meaningful idea: confidence can grow as we move towards the people and places that support us.

## Storytellers

### Reading — Threads of Home (403 words)

Jayden's notebook was crowded with descriptions of his neighbourhood. He had written about streetlights, basketball courts, passing cars and the smell of food drifting from family kitchens.
Some lines sounded beautiful:
Streetlights are golden moons above the road.
The evening wind whispers through open gates.
Laughter spills across the basketball court.
Yet when Jayden read the poem aloud, the images seemed to pull in different directions. His teacher asked what he wanted to say about his street.
“That it's busy,” he replied. Then he reconsidered. He remembered neighbours checking on one another during heavy rain, older students helping younger children cross the road and games that brought families together. “Actually, it's about people looking after each other.”
He examined a metaphor describing the neighbourhood as a giant machine. It suggested constant movement, but not the relationships he wanted to emphasise. He replaced it:
Our street is a woven mat,
Each family another thread.
The image suggested separate people connected through shared responsibilities. Jayden knew woven mats held significance in many Pacific families and communities, and he wanted to treat that connection thoughtfully rather than claim everyone's experience was identical.
He reorganised his poem into three stanzas. The first described the street's sounds. The second showed people offering help. The third returned to the woven mat:
Even when one thread loosens,
Others reach to hold it close.
His teacher asked whether the ending made the community seem too perfect. Jayden thought about disagreements between neighbours and the difficult circumstances some families faced. He added:
Some threads pull against each other,
Yet still we share the same design.
The new lines acknowledged tension without losing the central idea of belonging.
When Jayden read the draft aloud, he noticed that several long lines interrupted its rhythm. He shortened them. He also removed a simile comparing the basketball court to a roaring ocean. He liked that image, but it distracted from the woven-mat metaphor.
“A good line doesn't always belong in the final poem,” his teacher explained.
Jayden found that difficult. He had spent time creating the ocean comparison. But without it, the poem felt more connected.
He titled his final version Threads of Home. The poem was shorter than his first draft, yet it said more. Jayden had learned that strong imagery, structure and rhythm needed to work together. Revision sometimes meant letting go of a favourite line so that the poem's message could be heard.

## Legends

### Reading — Platform Between Two Worlds (584 words)

Mele believed good poetry depended on remarkable words. For her first complete poem, she collected striking comparisons: the moon as a silver coin, the city as a sleeping giant and the wind as a restless traveller. Each image sounded impressive alone. Together, they formed a poem without a clear direction.
During a writing conference, her teacher asked what the poem was about.
“Growing up,” Mele replied.
“What part of growing up?”
She hesitated. She wanted greater independence, yet she feared leaving familiar routines and expectations behind. The teacher suggested that this tension, rather than a collection of decorative images, could organise the poem.
Mele imagined standing on a railway platform while a train prepared to depart. The setting suggested movement, anticipation and uncertainty. She drafted:
Tomorrow is a train with open doors.
Its engine murmurs promises I cannot read.
Behind me, yesterday folds its familiar arms.
The train represented an unknown future. Yesterday's arms suggested both comfort and the difficulty of leaving familiar things. At last, her images belonged to the same idea.
Her teacher questioned the word “promises”. A promise implies some assurance. Mele realised that the future offered possibilities, not guarantees. She revised the line:
Its engine murmurs possibilities without names.
Next, she planned three stanzas: the approaching future, the familiar past and the moment of departure. Her first ending was confident:
I step aboard and never look behind.
But that line suggested growing up required rejecting the people who had supported her. She wanted independence without disconnection.
She tried another ending:
I carry their voices in my pockets.
The platform slips behind the glass.
I move forward without leaving them behind.
The last line explained her message, perhaps too completely. Removing it made the ending more open, but might suggest a permanent separation. Mele experimented again:
Their voices travel in the seat beside me.
Remembered voices could not literally occupy a train seat. Yet the personification suggested that relationships continued even as circumstances changed.
Before sharing the poem, Mele checked the rhythm of its three stanzas. Short lines made the train seem to pause; longer sentences carried the speaker towards departure. She kept one deliberate pause before the final image so the audience could feel the uncertainty. She also considered whether every reference to the past was negative. The warm, familiar arms mattered because they showed that attachment and independence could coexist. She decided not to explain the train symbol in the poem itself. Readers could work out its meaning from the platform, doors and movement, while her writer’s note could explain the choices behind it.
At a workshop, classmates interpreted the train differently. One saw adulthood; another saw educational opportunity. A third thought the platform represented family expectations. Mele could identify evidence for each reading. Their responses helped her recognise useful ambiguity: a poem could guide interpretation without fixing every meaning.
However, one student thought the speaker was escaping an unhappy home. Mele discovered why. An earlier draft had described yesterday's arms as “chains”. She meant the weight of familiarity, but chains implied imprisonment. Replacing them with “familiar arms” restored the balance of comfort and restriction.
By the final draft, Mele had removed more than half her original images. The poem was shorter, more coherent and more emotionally complex. She had discovered that unusual language was not automatically effective language. The best comparisons served the meaning she wanted to communicate.
Revision was not evidence that the first draft had failed. It was how Mele found the poem beneath the poem.
